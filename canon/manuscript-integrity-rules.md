# Manuscript Integrity Rules — Tattvashila

**Document Type:** Preservation Governance  
**Canonical Status:** Primary  
**Scope:** All manuscripts in the Tattvashila Granthālaya — present and future  
**Authority:** These rules govern all persons, systems, and processes that interact
with canonical manuscript content

---

## Why These Rules Exist

A philosophical manuscript is not a content asset. It is not a document that can
be optimized, simplified, restructured for a different audience, or updated to
remain current. It is a composed text — the product of a sustained process of
thinking that produced a specific arrangement of language, argument, and silence.

Every element of that arrangement is intentional. Repetition, when it occurs, is
deliberate — it is the text working through something slowly. Long sentences are
not failures of concision — they are the movement of thought that cannot be
broken without losing what the thought was doing. Pauses between paragraphs are
not inefficiencies — they are the time the reader requires to stay with what
was said before moving forward.

Integrity rules exist because systems — editorial systems, rendering systems,
AI systems, translation workflows, publishing conventions — are trained to
optimize. They are trained to shorten, simplify, clarify, modernize, and
make accessible. When these impulses are applied to philosophical manuscripts
without governance, the manuscript is gradually destroyed — not through any
single catastrophic act, but through the accumulation of small, well-intentioned
interventions that, one by one, remove what was difficult, slow, or unfamiliar.

These rules name what must not happen.

---

## I. The Absolute Prohibitions

These prohibitions apply without exception to all manuscripts in the Granthālaya.
They cannot be overridden by editorial preference, accessibility concerns,
technical convenience, or any other consideration.

### No Simplification

The text may not be rewritten at a lower reading level.  
The text may not be restructured for a general audience.  
The text may not have its vocabulary replaced with simpler synonyms.  
The text may not be "clarified" by adding explanatory sentences not in the original.

If the text is difficult, the difficulty belongs to it.

### No Conversational Rewriting

The text may not be rewritten in a conversational register.  
The text may not have its formality reduced.  
The text may not have contractions added where the original does not use them.  
The text may not have its sentence structures broken into shorter, more casual units.

The register of the text is part of the text.

### No Motivational Reframing

The text may not be reframed to appear encouraging, empowering, or actionable.  
The text may not have its conclusions repositioned as practical takeaways.  
The text may not be summarized in ways that imply immediate applicability.  
The text may not be described as "helpful for" any particular practical outcome.

Philosophical inquiry is not motivational content.

### No Shortening of Intentional Repetition

Where the manuscript repeats itself — a phrase, a question, a structural pattern —
the repetition must be preserved in full.

Repetition in philosophical prose is rarely accidental. It is the text returning
to something it has not yet finished with. It is the text building through
reiteration rather than through linear progression.

Any editor or system that removes repetition because it appears redundant has
misread the text.

### No Terminology Replacement

No canonical philosophical term may be replaced with a simpler English word.  
No Sanskrit or Hindi term may be substituted with a casual modern equivalent.  
No term defined in the Canonical Lexicon may be used outside its defined semantic
boundaries.

The Canonical Lexicon governs terminology. The manuscript governs itself.

### No Modern Content Optimization

The text may not be structured for search engine optimization.  
The text may not have keywords added or emphasized.  
The text may not be segmented for digital content distribution.  
The text may not have headlines, subheadings, or bullets added where the original
does not have them.  
The text may not be adapted for social media distribution.  
The text may not be excerpted without institutional authorization.

## II. Editing Permissions — What Is Permitted

These rules govern what editing is permitted on canonical manuscripts. The list
is intentionally narrow.

### Permitted: Typographic Correction

Typographic errors — spelling, punctuation, diacritical marks — may be corrected
when they are clearly errors and not intentional choices. Corrections must be
documented and reviewed by the institution.

**Important:** Unconventional punctuation and sentence structure that appears
to be an error may in fact be intentional. Correction must be sanctioned before
it is made.

### Permitted: Diacritical Standardization

Diacritical marks and transliteration may be standardized to IAST across the
manuscript for consistency. This is not a content change; it is a rendering
standardization.

### Permitted: Rendering-Layer Annotation

For web rendering, metadata used for institutional purposes (publication date,
composition state, Granthālaya position) may be added at the rendering layer.
This metadata must not appear within the manuscript reading view. It belongs
to the archive index, not the text.

### Permitted: Structural Markup for Rendering

The manuscript structure (chapters, sections, paragraphs) may be marked up
for rendering systems. This markup is a rendering-layer convention — it does
not alter the text and must not be visible to the reader.

## III. AI System Governance

AI systems present a specific and serious integrity risk to manuscripts. This
section addresses that risk directly.

### The Nature of the Risk

AI language systems are trained on large corpora of text. They are trained to
assist, improve, clarify, complete, summarize, and paraphrase. These are their
default behaviors — and they are appropriate in many contexts.

They are not appropriate in the context of canonical philosophical manuscripts.

An AI system asked to "improve" a manuscript passage will produce something
smoother, clearer, and more immediately accessible. What it produces will not
be the manuscript. It will be the manuscript rewritten for contemporary reading
preferences — with its difficulties removed, its repetition compressed, and its
philosophical cadence replaced by the rhythm of readable contemporary prose.

This is not improvement. It is a different text.

### Governing Rules for AI System Interaction with Manuscripts

**AI systems must not:**

1. Rewrite, paraphrase, or simplify any passage of a canonical manuscript
2. Generate "improved" versions of manuscript prose
3. Summarize manuscript content for any purpose other than archive-level indexing
4. Complete manuscript passages that are incomplete or provisional
5. Suggest alternative phrasings for philosophical terms
6. Apply "clarity improvements" to manuscript prose
7. Translate canonical manuscripts without explicit institutional authorization

**AI systems may:**

1. Render canonical manuscripts for web display, following the Rendering Constitution
2. Mark up manuscript structure for rendering purposes
3. Reference manuscript content with exact quotation in institutional documents
4. Apply formatting conventions (paragraph spacing, heading hierarchy) as specified
   in the Rendering Constitution
5. Flag potential typographic errors for human review — without making corrections
   automatically

### The Canonical Lexicon as a Constraint

AI systems interacting with manuscript content must treat the Canonical Lexicon
as a constraint, not as a reference. Every canonical term defined in the lexicon
must be used exactly as defined — not paraphrased, not synonymized, not "made
more accessible."

When an AI system encounters a term in the Canonical Lexicon, it must:

1. Use the term exactly as defined
2. Not substitute an English equivalent
3. Not explain the term within the running prose (explanations belong to the
   canonical lexicon, not the manuscript)
4. Not modify the semantic context in which the term appears

### Subordination of Rendering Systems

All rendering systems — including AI systems used for rendering — are subordinate
to the canonical manuscript. The manuscript is the authority. The rendering system
exists to serve it.

When a conflict arises between what a rendering system would naturally produce
and what the manuscript requires, the manuscript requirement governs.

## IV. Translation Governance

### The Canonical Language

The canonical language of Tattvashila's manuscripts is Hindi with Sanskrit-rooted
philosophical terminology. All granthas are composed primarily in Hindi.

### Translation as Interpretation

A translation of a canonical manuscript is an interpretive act — it is not a
rendering of the same text in a different language. It is a new text that attempts
to carry the philosophical content of the original into a language with different
philosophical conventions, semantic structures, and cultural contexts.

Translations are therefore:

- Always secondary to the original
- Explicitly marked as interpretive
- Authorized by the institution before release
- Not used as substitute canonical sources
- Not used to replace the original in any institutional context

### English Translations

An English translation of Tattvaprashna or any Tattvashila grantha may eventually
be composed. When it is:

1. It will be designated as an "interpretive canonical translation" — not
   a literal translation
2. It will be composed by persons with deep familiarity with the philosophical
   tradition and the specific semantic terrain of the canonical lexicon
3. It will be reviewed by the institution against the original
4. It will appear in the Granthālaya alongside the original — not replacing it
5. It will carry its own canonical source-of-truth document, noting its relationship
   to the Hindi original

## V. Quotation and Excerption

### Quotation from Manuscripts

Quotation from canonical manuscripts — for use in institutional communications,
editorial notes, or other publications — must follow these rules:

1. **Exact quotation only.** No paraphrase presented as quotation.
2. **Contextual completeness.** A quotation must include sufficient context that
   the meaning is not distorted by isolation.
3. **Attribution.** Every quotation must be attributed to the canonical source
   (manuscript title, chapter, section — when the text is formally published).
4. **No editorial modification.** No ellipsis used to elide a passage that changes
   the meaning. No word changed for grammatical convenience without marking.

### Excerption for Publication

Excerpts of manuscripts for external publication require institutional authorization.

An excerpt is not a sample, a preview, or a marketing fragment. If excerpts are
authorized, they must:

- Be selected by the institution, not by the publication
- Be presented with the full canonical title and composition context
- Not be edited for length, clarity, or audience
- Not be accompanied by summaries or explanatory paraphrases unless also authorized

## VI. Versioning and Revision

### Composition Versions

During composition, a manuscript may exist in multiple internal versions. These
are not canonical and do not leave the institution's custody.

### The Released Version

When a manuscript is placed in the Granthālaya, one version is released. That
version is the canonical text. It does not receive updates, revisions, or
corrections without a formal institutional review process.

### Substantive Revision

If an error in the canonical text — factual, philosophical, or typographic —
requires correction after release:

1. The nature of the correction is documented
2. The correction is sanctioned by the institution
3. The revision is noted in the manuscript's source-of-truth document
4. If the revision is substantive (philosophical, not merely typographic), the
   revised version is dated and noted as a revised edition

A revised edition does not replace the original. Both remain in the archive.

### No Continuous Updating

Manuscripts in the Granthālaya are not updated to remain current, topical,
or consistent with positions that may have evolved since composition. A manuscript
is a record of thinking at the time of its completion. It does not retroactively
absorb new positions.

If a position changes substantially, a new manuscript is composed — not the
existing one revised.
