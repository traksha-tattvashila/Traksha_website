# Rendering Fidelity Rules — Tattvashila

**Document Type:** Rendering Safety Governance  
**Canonical Status:** Primary  
**Scope:** All rendering processes for Tattvashila canonical manuscripts  
**Authority:** These rules govern every system, person, and process that translates
canonical manuscript source into any rendered form

---

## The Central Rule

The rendering of a manuscript must be indistinguishable from the manuscript itself
in everything that matters: paragraph structure, sentence boundaries, contemplative
pacing, semantic rhythm, and the silences the author placed within and between
passages of prose.

Rendering is not production. Rendering is not editing. Rendering is not adaptation.

Rendering is the act of making the manuscript visible — and the visible form must
be the manuscript. Not a version of it. Not a cleaned version. Not an improved version.
The manuscript.

---

## I. Structural Preservation Requirements

### 1.1 Paragraph Boundaries

Every paragraph boundary in the canonical source must be preserved exactly in the
rendered output.

A paragraph boundary is a deliberate act of the author — a decision that the thought
in progress is complete and that what follows is a new beginning. It is not a
formatting convention. It is the manuscript's rhythm made visible.

**The following paragraph-boundary violations are prohibited:**

**Paragraph merging** — combining two distinct paragraphs into one, for any reason:
because they appear to address the same topic, because the transition between them
seems smooth, because the resulting paragraph seems more unified, because a rendering
system's default behavior merges short paragraphs. Merging destroys the author's
decision to pause.

**Paragraph splitting** — dividing one paragraph into two or more, for any reason:
because the paragraph is long, because it appears to contain two distinct ideas,
because a rendering system's line-break detection misreads structure as paragraph
division. Splitting destroys the author's decision to sustain.

**Paragraph reordering** — moving paragraphs within a section or chapter for any
reason: because the reordering seems more logical, because a summary paragraph
appears better suited as an introduction, because a rendering system rearranges
sections to create visual balance. The order is the argument.

### 1.2 Sentence Boundaries

Every sentence boundary — marked by the author's original punctuation — must be
preserved in the rendered output.

Sentence boundary violations include:

**Sentence merging** — combining two sentences into one using a comma, semicolon,
or conjunction when the original uses a period. The period is the author's decision
to stop.

**Sentence splitting** — dividing one sentence into two using a period when the
original is a single sentence. Long sentences in philosophical prose are not
failures of concision. They are the movement of thought through a complex
consideration that cannot be broken without losing the arc of the thought.

**Sentence restructuring** — changing the syntactic order of a sentence to make
it more grammatically conventional. The author's syntax is part of the meaning.
An inverted sentence is inverted for a reason.

### 1.3 Intentional Repetition

Repetition in philosophical prose is not an error. It is a method.

When a term, phrase, or structural pattern repeats within a passage, the repetition
is intentional — the text is returning to something it has not yet finished examining,
or it is building meaning through reiteration rather than through linear progression.

**Repetition must be preserved in full.** Any rendering system that flags repetition
as an error, suggests its removal, or automatically compresses it has been allowed to
function as an editor. This is not permitted.

### 1.4 Contemplative Pacing

The pacing of philosophical prose — the tempo at which ideas are introduced, held,
and released — is encoded in the prose itself: in sentence length, paragraph length,
the density of ideas per paragraph, and the presence of short paragraphs following
long ones.

Rendering must not alter pacing by:

- Normalizing paragraph length (making short paragraphs longer or long paragraphs shorter)
- Adding transitional sentences between paragraphs that the author did not write
- Removing short paragraphs that appear to be "too brief" to function as standalone units
- Adding summaries or recaps at the end of sections

### 1.5 Semantic Silence

Semantic silence refers to what the author chose not to say — pauses, absences,
and the space between ideas that allows the reader to stay with what was said.

Rendering must not fill semantic silence by:

- Adding explanatory sentences to "clarify" what the previous paragraph meant
- Inserting transitional phrases that smooth over a deliberate gap
- Appending "in other words" restatements of what the prose already said
- Adding introductory sentences before a chapter or section that the author began
  without preamble

## II. Explicit Prohibitions

These prohibitions apply to all rendering processes — human, automated, and AI-mediated.
They cannot be overridden by time pressure, technical constraints, or editorial preference.

### No Paragraph Merging

Already stated in I.1. Stated again here because it is the most common rendering violation.

### No Paragraph Splitting

Already stated in I.1. Stated again here because long paragraphs are frequently
misread as formatting errors when they are philosophical structure.

### No AI Cleanup Behavior

AI systems have trained behaviors that activate by default when processing text:
they smooth awkward syntax, normalize punctuation, remove repetition, balance
sentence length, and clarify ambiguous passages. These behaviors are described
by the systems as "cleanup" or "improvement."

None of these behaviors are permitted when rendering canonical manuscripts.

The specific AI cleanup behaviors that are prohibited:

- Grammar correction that changes sentence structure
- Punctuation normalization that changes the author's rhythm
- Word substitution for "clarity" or "naturalness"
- Passive-voice conversion
- Redundancy removal
- Any form of "enhancement" to the source prose

### No Readability Rewriting

Readability scores, reading-level assessments, and clarity metrics have no
authority over the manuscript. A passage that scores as "difficult" is difficult
because the thought it contains is difficult. The rendering system does not
make it easier. The manuscript does what it does.

### No Improved Phrasing

There is no improved phrasing of a canonical passage. There is the passage, and
there is corruption of the passage. These are the only two states.

Any system that offers "alternative phrasings" for manuscript sentences has been
allowed to function as a co-author. This is not permitted.

### No Semantic Optimization

Semantic optimization is the process of adjusting language to communicate more
efficiently, more clearly, or more precisely than the original. It is not permitted
because the original is already as efficient, clear, and precise as the author
intended it to be. The rendering system's judgment about efficiency and clarity
is not authoritative.

### No Emotional Amplification

Emotional amplification is the process of strengthening the affective tone of a
passage — making it more moving, more inspiring, more urgent, or more consoling
than the original. It is not permitted because the philosophical prose of the
Granthālaya is not written to produce emotional effects. It is written to produce
understanding. Amplifying the emotional register changes what the text is doing.

## III. What Rendering Is Permitted to Do

Having stated what rendering must not do, this section states what it is permitted
to do — because the permissions are as important as the prohibitions.

### Permitted: Typographic Presentation

Rendering may apply the typography specified in the Rendering Constitution. This
includes: typeface, size, line height, paragraph spacing, chapter opening hierarchy,
Devanagari rendering, margin width, and color system.

Typographic presentation serves the manuscript. It does not alter it.

### Permitted: Structural Markup

Rendering may apply structural markup (HTML, markdown, or other format-specific
conventions) to indicate: chapter boundaries, section boundaries, paragraph
divisions, chapter headings, Devanagari passages, transliterated passages.

Structural markup is a rendering convention — it does not appear to the reader
and does not alter the text.

### Permitted: Navigation Elements

Rendering may add navigation elements at the beginning and end of a manuscript
chapter: a return link to the Granthālaya, a chapter title, a chapter numeral.
These elements are rendering conventions — they are not part of the manuscript
and must not appear within the manuscript's prose.

### Permitted: Canonical Metadata

Rendering may attach canonical metadata to the rendered output: manuscript title,
Devanagari title, composition state, Granthālaya position. This metadata appears
in the Granthālaya index — not within the manuscript reading view.

## IV. The Rendering System's Role

The rendering system exists in a position of strict subordination to the canonical
manuscript. This is not a technical limitation — it is an institutional principle.

The rendering system does not know the manuscript better than the manuscript knows
itself. The rendering system cannot improve what was composed with deliberate care.
The rendering system's defaults, trained behaviors, and aesthetic preferences are
all irrelevant to the question of what the manuscript requires.

The rendering system's role is: to make the manuscript visible with perfect fidelity.

When the rendering system produces output that differs from the canonical source
in any of the dimensions governed by these rules, the output is a failed rendering —
regardless of how good it looks, how readable it appears, or how technically
sophisticated the system that produced it.

A failed rendering is not published. It is corrected.
