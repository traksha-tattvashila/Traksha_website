# Rendering Constitution — Tattvashila Granthālaya

**Document Type:** Permanent Rendering Governance  
**Canonical Status:** Primary  
**Scope:** All Tattvashila manuscript renderings — web, print, and any future medium  
**Authority:** Subordinate to the canonical manuscript; governing over all rendering systems

---

## Foundational Principle

Rendering exists to preserve the manuscript — not to interpret it.

Every typographic decision, spacing choice, visual hierarchy, and interface behaviour
must serve one purpose: to allow the manuscript to be read as it was composed. The
rendering system is a transparent vessel. It must not assert itself, impose its own
aesthetic, or optimize for any dimension that is not fidelity to the text.

A rendering constitution is necessary because systems drift. Typography trends shift.
Web interfaces accumulate interaction patterns borrowed from commercial publishing,
social platforms, and content consumption products. Without a permanent governing
document, future rendering decisions will unconsciously borrow from these contexts —
and the manuscript will gradually become something it is not.

This constitution prevents that drift.

---

## I. Typography Principles

### 1.1 Type Scale

The manuscript is rendered in a type scale that communicates institutional gravity
and manuscript tradition. The scale prioritizes reading comfort at length, not
typographic expressiveness or visual hierarchy for scanning.

The canonical type scale for manuscript rendering:

| Usage | Size Range | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Running prose | 18–20px / 1.12–1.25rem | Reading weight; not too large |
| Chapter openings | display — restrained | Quiet authority |
| Section headings | Between display and prose | No visual urgency |
| Marginalia, footnotes | Reduced, quiet | Not suppressed — present |
| Devanagari script | Must match prose weight optically | Not decorative |

**What the scale must not do:**
- Compress type for density (information-product pattern)
- Expand type for impact (marketing-headline pattern)
- Use multiple weights for visual interest
- Introduce color into type for hierarchy

### 1.2 Typeface Selection

The canonical typeface system follows the Tattvashila editorial system established
in the web platform:

**Prose type:** A serif typeface of manuscript character — not a geometric serif,
not a transitional serif optimized for screen scanning, not a humanist sans-serif.
The typeface must carry reading gravity appropriate to sustained philosophical text.

**Display type:** The same family as prose, used at larger scale — not a display
typeface distinct from the prose family. Unity of typographic voice is non-negotiable.

**Devanagari type:** A typeface with genuine calligraphic grounding. Not a
geometric Devanagari optimized for UI. Not a digital approximation of printed
Devanagari. The script must be rendered with the weight and presence it carries
in manuscript tradition.

**What typefaces must not be used:**
- Geometric sans-serifs (no matter how refined)
- Screen-optimized serif typefaces designed for dashboard or editorial content products
- Display typefaces selected for visual personality
- Typefaces associated with contemporary publishing or media brands

### 1.3 Weight and Style

The manuscript renders in a single weight: regular.

**Permitted emphases:**
- Italic — used sparingly, only for terms requiring semantic distinction
- Small capitals — for chapter numerals and institutional labels only
- Devanagari at native weight — not bold, not light

**Prohibited emphases:**
- Bold within running prose
- Underline for anything other than hyperlinks (and hyperlinks within manuscript
  prose should be extremely rare)
- Colored type for emphasis
- Weight contrast for visual interest

### 1.4 Devanagari Rendering

Devanagari must be rendered with the following principles:

1. **Script before transliteration.** Wherever Devanagari can be rendered, it appears
   first. Transliteration follows, if at all.
2. **Same optical weight as prose.** Devanagari must not appear decorative, smaller,
   or as a subtitle to transliteration.
3. **No romanization replacement.** Romanized transliteration does not replace
   Devanagari — it supplements it where necessary.
4. **IAST standard for transliteration.** No simplified romanization that loses
   diacritical distinctions.

## II. Spacing Philosophy

### 2.1 Line Height

Manuscript prose is rendered with generous line height — not to create whitespace
as a design element, but because philosophical prose requires breathing room between
lines for genuine reading. The reader must be able to follow a long sentence without
losing their place in a compressed text block.

Canonical line height for running prose: 1.75–1.90 (unitless ratio)

This is not the line height of web content. It is the line height of a manuscript
being read carefully.

### 2.2 Paragraph Spacing

Paragraphs are separated by measured vertical space — enough to mark the conclusion
of one unit of thought before the next begins. Not so much that the text fragments
into visual sections.

The paragraph spacing must reinforce the sense that each paragraph is complete in
itself — not a unit in a content feed, but a unit of sustained thought.

**What paragraph spacing must not do:**
- Create the rhythm of a blog post (too much spacing; fragments content)
- Compress paragraphs together (content-density pattern)
- Use first-line indentation in place of paragraph spacing (print convention that
  creates visual ambiguity on screen)

### 2.3 Chapter and Section Opening Spacing

Chapter openings receive significant vertical space before the first line of prose.
This space is not decorative. It marks the transition from navigation or context
into the text itself — a pause before beginning.

The space before a chapter opening should be sufficient that the reader feels
the manuscript has begun — not that the next section has appeared.

### 2.4 Margin and Measure

The text column width (measure) governs reading comfort for long prose. Canonical
measure for manuscript prose: 60–72 characters per line.

This is narrower than many web layouts. The choice is deliberate: a measure
appropriate to reading, not to screen-filling. Wide measures create line tracking
difficulty in long philosophical sentences. The manuscript column is narrow enough
to be read comfortably.

Margins outside the text column are not wasted space. They create the sense that
the text is placed within a larger quiet — not pushed to the edges of the screen.

## III. Silence Architecture

Silence in the manuscript rendering context means: deliberate, governed absence.

The rendering must include three kinds of silence:

### 3.1 Typographic Silence

Silence between sections that is not filled with ornament, dividers, or transitional
elements. The section simply ends. The next section begins. The space between them
is unmarked — not nothing, but unmarked space.

Where ornamental dividers are used, they must be:
- Minimal (a single rule, a simple mark)
- Quiet in weight (not heavier than the prose)
- Used sparingly (not after every section)

### 3.2 Interface Silence

No interface element should compete for attention with the manuscript text.

This means:
- Navigation collapses or disappears during manuscript reading
- No sidebar content during prose sections
- No progress indicators (reading progress bars are content-consumption metrics)
- No share buttons, recommendation panels, or "what to read next" suggestions
  within the manuscript view
- No timestamps or publication metadata within the reading flow

### 3.3 Visual Silence

The manuscript is rendered on a quiet background — warm neutral, not stark white,
not dark mode by default. The background must communicate that reading is occurring
in a settled space, not a screen.

No background imagery, texture, or color variation within the manuscript reading view.

## IV. Chapter-Opening Systems

### 4.1 Chapter Opening Elements

Each chapter opening carries, in sequence:

1. The chapter numeral — in Sanskrit numeral or Roman numeral form (not Arabic)
2. The chapter title — in Hindi, with Devanagari script
3. The Sanskrit or traditional equivalent — below the title, in smaller size, italic
4. A single opening line of white space — sufficient to begin reading

### 4.2 What Chapter Openings Must Not Contain

- Pull quotes from the chapter
- Summary statements or introductory paragraphs added by editors
- Running chapter descriptions
- Visual imagery (unless the manuscript itself calls for it)
- Navigation back to contents within the opening

### 4.3 The First Line of Prose

The first line of each chapter must appear at the same typographic weight as all
subsequent prose. No drop caps. No large-size opening words. No "first paragraph"
styling that separates the opening from the rest.

The manuscript begins quietly. It does not announce itself.

## V. Navigation and Wayfinding Within Manuscripts

### 5.1 In-Text Navigation

Navigation within a manuscript is permitted only at two moments:

1. **At the very beginning** — a table of contents or chapter list, before the text begins
2. **At the very end** — a return path to the Granthālaya index

Navigation must not appear within the manuscript reading flow.

### 5.2 Chapter Progression

Movement between chapters follows the sequence of the manuscript — not a "next
recommended text" algorithm, not a chapter-selection grid, not a contents panel
that persists through reading.

A reader moves forward through the manuscript as the manuscript was composed.

### 5.3 Absence of Metrics

The following must not appear anywhere within the manuscript reading view:

- Word count
- Reading time estimate
- Progress percentage
- Page count (unless the manuscript is formally paginated)
- Publication date (belongs in the archive index, not the reading view)
- Author biographical information mid-text

## VI. Ornament Restraint

Ornamental elements in the manuscript rendering are governed by a rule of
maximum restraint:

**Permitted:**
- A single horizontal rule between major sections, when the transition requires marking
- The institutional mark (तत्त्वशिला / Tattvashila) at the close of the manuscript

**Permitted only in specific contexts:**
- A simple decorative mark between chapters — only if the manuscript's own
  tradition calls for it; not by default
- Devanagari ornamental characters — only where the manuscript itself includes them

**Never permitted:**
- Decorative borders around text blocks
- Flourishes at chapter headings
- Photographic or illustrative content within manuscript prose, unless explicitly
  part of the manuscript
- Color as an ornamental element
- Pattern or texture as background

---

## VII. Archival Composition Rhythm

The manuscript is composed in a particular rhythm — the rhythm of thought that
proceeds carefully, pauses, returns, and continues. The rendering must not
interrupt this rhythm.

Specific rendering behaviors that interrupt compositional rhythm:

- **Scroll-triggered animations** — text that appears on scroll, fades in, or
  otherwise draws attention to itself as it enters the view. The manuscript
  was composed for reading, not for visual choreography.
- **Highlighting or annotation defaults** — interface annotations that appear
  by default, drawing the reader's eye away from the text.
- **Related text suggestions** — appearing mid-read, breaking the compositional
  continuity.
- **Auto-scroll or reading-speed assistants** — tools that mechanize the reading
  pace.

Reveal animations (subtle fade-in for page load) are permitted on initial page
entry only. Once reading has begun, the text is still.

---

## VIII. Print and Future Medium Considerations

This constitution governs web rendering primarily. For print and future media:

### 8.1 Print

Print rendering of a Tattvashila manuscript requires a separate print-specific
typographic study. This constitution governs the following in print:

- The type hierarchy, weight, and spacing principles carry across
- The silence architecture applies: no visual decoration not in the manuscript
- The margin principles apply: wide enough for quiet, not compressed for economy
- Devanagari rendering principles apply

### 8.2 Future Digital Formats

No future format (ebook, audio, video reading) may be treated as a primary
canonical form. The canonical form is the manuscript text itself. All other
formats are derived and must be sanctioned by the institution.

Audio readings must preserve: pace, pause, and the contemplative rhythm of the
prose. They must not be produced to content-consumption platform standards.
